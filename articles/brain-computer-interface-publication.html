Intelligent Movement-Controlled Brain-Computer Interface System Based on EEG Signal

A published book chapter examining EEG acquisition, preprocessing, feature selection, and machine-learning classification for a movement-controlled brain-computer interface.

Khin Eaindray Htun · May Thu Kyaw · Htun Htun · Kyaw Kyaw Oo · Si Thu Aung · Md. Mehedi Hassan River Publishers · Pages 211–233

This chapter investigates how EEG signals can be processed and classified as control inputs for a movement-oriented brain-computer interface. It follows the research pipeline from signal acquisition and dataset preparation through preprocessing, feature selection, classification, evaluation, and model comparison.

The research problem

Brain-computer interfaces translate patterns in neural activity into commands that an external system can interpret. EEG offers a non-invasive way to capture those patterns, but raw signals require careful preparation before a classifier can distinguish intended movement categories reliably.

What the chapter covers

The chapter documents EEG data acquisition, the dataset used for the study, preprocessing, feature-selection methods, and neural-network classification. Its results section evaluates classification accuracy and compares models before discussing strengths, limitations, and future research directions.

Why the work matters

The work connects physiological-signal processing with practical machine-learning evaluation. Rather than treating classification as an isolated model task, it frames performance as the outcome of an end-to-end pipeline: signal capture, representation, feature choice, training, comparison, and interpretation.

Publication context

The chapter appears in Brain Networks in Neuroscience: Personalization Unveiled Via Artificial Intelligence, a 2025 River Publishers volume on the intersection of neuroscience, brain networks, and artificial intelligence. It is Chapter 10 of the collection and spans pages 211–233.

Research perspective

For this portfolio, the publication represents an early research milestone at the intersection of AI and human-centered sensing. Its structure—acquire, preprocess, select, classify, compare, and examine limitations—continues to inform later work in computer vision, autonomous systems, and applied machine learning.
